## Part B: Reflection

**What I did.** I recorded daily stand-ups, sprint reviews and retrospectives for the whole placement, so there is a clear record of what I planned, what I did, and what blocked me.

**What I learned.** The logbook turned vague memories ("I think we changed that last week") into facts. Writing "Yesterday, Today, Blockers" each day made me notice problems early, and the retrospectives showed me a pattern: my estimates were usually too low for testing and security work. Because I work alone, the logbook acted like a teammate who asks "what is in your way?" each morning.

**What was difficult.** Keeping the habit going on busy development days. When I skipped entries, I had to reconstruct them from my work and my commits.

**What I would do differently.** I would write the stand-up at the *start* of every day, take a screenshot of the Trello board at the end of each sprint, and record real hours so the logbook and sprint backlog agree.

## Connecting the artefacts

The five artefacts form one story:

1. The **Agile Team Charter** set the purpose, scope and working rules.
2. The **User Stories and Tasks** turned the purpose into small, testable pieces of work.
3. The **Product Backlog** ordered those pieces by value and changed with each demo.
4. The **Sprint Backlog** planned each week's slice of the backlog within my real capacity.
5. The **Agile Logbook** recorded what actually happened, including feedback and lessons.

Together they show how the **Collect, Select, Reflect, Connect** cycle played out on a real project: collecting my planning and tracking evidence, selecting the strongest artefacts, reflecting on each one, and connecting them into a single picture of how I plan, deliver and improve work in an Agile environment. The biggest overall lesson is that Agile is mostly about **communication and feedback**. The weekly demo with my mentor shaped my backlog, my sprint plans and my logbook more than any single tool did.
